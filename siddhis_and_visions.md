# Chapter IV: Siddhis and Visions

*Pages 38--48*

## 1. Understanding the Main Teaching

The central teaching of this chapter is:

**Do not mistake extraordinary spiritual experiences for spiritual
realization.**

During meditation, yoga, or other spiritual practices, people sometimes
report unusual experiences, such as:

-   Seeing bright lights or different colours.
-   Experiencing visions of gods, saints, or divine figures.
-   Hearing unusual sounds.
-   Feeling that the body has become extremely light.
-   Experiencing sensations of leaving the physical body.
-   Developing unusual abilities or powers.

Some people consider these experiences signs of spiritual progress.

However, Ramana Maharshi makes an important distinction:

**Having an extraordinary experience does not mean that you have
understood your true nature.**

You may experience something unusual during meditation and still remain
controlled by anger, fear, jealousy, pride, and attachment.

Spiritual realization is not about experiencing something extraordinary.
It is about discovering who you really are.

## 2. What Are Siddhis?

*Siddhis* refers to supernatural or extraordinary powers traditionally
associated with advanced yogic practices.

Some examples include:

-   Clairvoyance -- supposedly seeing things beyond ordinary physical
    perception.
-   Telepathy -- supposedly understanding another person's thoughts.
-   Levitation -- supposedly floating above the ground.
-   Extraordinary physical abilities.
-   Knowledge of events beyond normal sensory perception.

In traditional Indian spiritual literature, such powers are sometimes
described as results of intense meditation and spiritual discipline.

But Ramana Maharshi asks us to consider a more fundamental question:

**Who wants these powers?**

Suppose someone develops an unusual ability through meditation.

Instead of becoming free from the ego, that person might start thinking:

-   I am more powerful than others.
-   I am spiritually superior.
-   I have achieved something extraordinary.
-   Other people should respect me.

Notice what has happened.

The spiritual practice has strengthened the person's ego rather than
dissolved it.

The person has acquired something extraordinary but has not necessarily
become free.

### Ramana's perspective

Ramana redirects attention from the power to the person seeking the
power.

Instead of asking:

"What extraordinary ability can I develop?"

Ask:

"Who is the person who wants this ability?"

Investigate that person.

This is closely connected to Ramana's central teaching of *Self-enquiry
(Who am I?)*.

## 3. Why Are Visions Not Necessarily Spiritual Realization?

A vision is an unusual experience that appears in the mind.

For example, during meditation, you might see:

-   A bright golden light.
-   A divine figure.
-   A temple.
-   A beautiful landscape.
-   A spiritual teacher.

Such experiences can feel incredibly real.

They may create happiness, peace, excitement, or even tears.

But Ramana asks us not to confuse the emotional intensity of an
experience with ultimate truth.

Consider this example.

Two people meditate.

**Person A:** - Sees a bright light. - Feels extremely happy. - Believes
that something divine has happened. - Becomes proud of the experience.

**Person B:** - Does not experience any unusual vision. - Simply
observes the mind. - Gradually becomes less attached to possessions. -
Responds to difficult situations with less anger. - Becomes less
concerned about personal recognition.

From the perspective of Ramana's teaching, Person B may be making more
meaningful spiritual progress.

Why?

Because spiritual progress is reflected in the weakening of ego and
attachment, not in the number of unusual experiences.

### An important distinction

A vision is something you experience.

Self-realization is the recognition of the awareness in which all
experiences appear.

One is an experience; the other concerns the very nature of the
experiencer.

## 4. Cohen's Perspective on Siddhis

Paul Brunton's account of Ramana Maharshi and the teachings recorded by
Arthur Osborne and other disciples are sometimes discussed alongside the
writings of Major A. W. Chadwick (also known as Sadhu Arunachala).

In the context of this chapter, Cohen takes a position that deserves
attention.

He does not simply dismiss every traditional account of extraordinary
powers as impossible.

He accepts that unusual phenomena have been described in spiritual
traditions.

However, he strongly opposes:

1.  Pursuing powers as the objective of spiritual practice.
2.  Exhibiting powers to attract followers.
3.  Using unusual experiences to establish spiritual superiority.
4.  Assuming that possessing powers means someone has attained
    liberation.

The important distinction is between the possibility of an unusual
phenomenon and its spiritual significance.

Even if someone possesses extraordinary abilities, that alone does not
establish Self-realization.

**A person can have extraordinary powers and still remain bound by the
ego.**

## 5. A Practical Example from Everyday Life

Imagine that you regularly meditate for 30 minutes.

One morning, you suddenly experience a bright light between your
eyebrows.

You feel peaceful and excited.

Your mind immediately starts interpreting the experience:

-   Perhaps my third eye has opened.
-   Perhaps I am reaching a higher spiritual level.
-   Perhaps I am progressing faster than other people.

Over the following days, you start waiting for that experience to happen
again.

You become disappointed when it doesn't happen.

Now observe what has happened.

Initially, meditation was an opportunity to quieten the mind.

But now the mind has created a new desire: experiencing that light
again.

You have replaced ordinary worldly desires with spiritual desires.

The attachment is still there. Only the object of attachment has
changed.

### What would Ramana's approach suggest?

Instead of chasing the experience, ask:

-   Who saw the light?
-   Who is excited about this experience?
-   Who wants to experience it again?
-   Can I observe the experience without becoming attached to it?

The purpose is not to suppress the experience.

Simply recognize that it is another experience appearing in awareness.

Let it come and go.

## 6. The Deeper Connection: Experiences vs. Experiencer

This is probably the most important philosophical idea in this chapter.

Think about your daily life.

You experience many things:

  Experience            What changes?
  --------------------- ------------------------
  Happiness             Your emotional state
  Sadness               Your emotional state
  Dreams                Your mental experience
  Meditation visions    Your mental experience
  Physical sensations   Your bodily experience
  Thoughts              Your mental activity

All these experiences continuously change.

But something is aware of these changes.

For example:

Yesterday, you were happy.

Today, you might be sad.

Tomorrow, you might be excited.

The emotional conditions change, but you are aware of each condition.

Ramana directs attention towards this awareness rather than towards the
changing experiences.

His fundamental question is:

**Who am I, before identifying myself with the body, thoughts, emotions,
and experiences?**

This is not an invitation to find another extraordinary experience.

It is an invitation to investigate the very source of the feeling "I".

## 7. How to Apply This Teaching in Meditation

You don't need to change your existing meditation practice.

You can incorporate this chapter's teaching into it.

Try the following:

**Step 1: Sit quietly.**

Allow your breathing to settle naturally.

**Step 2: Observe whatever appears.**

Thoughts, sounds, bodily sensations, peaceful feelings, or even unusual
visual experiences may arise.

Don't deliberately chase or suppress them.

**Step 3: Recognize that experiences come and go.**

A peaceful feeling may disappear.

A thought may arise.

A sound may interrupt your concentration.

Simply observe.

**Step 4: Turn attention towards the experiencer.**

Gently investigate:

"Who is aware of this thought?"

"Who is experiencing this peace?"

Don't try to produce an intellectual answer.

Remain with the question and turn attention towards the sense of "I".

**Step 5: Don't measure your progress through experiences.**

Don't judge your meditation by how peaceful you felt, how long you
remained without thoughts, or whether you saw something unusual.

The purpose is to understand the one who is experiencing all these
things.

## 8. Important Lessons from This Chapter

There are five important lessons:

1.  **Experiences are temporary:** Even extraordinary spiritual visions
    eventually appear and disappear.

2.  **Powers are not liberation:** Having unusual abilities does not
    necessarily remove ego, attachment, or ignorance.

3.  **Don't develop spiritual pride:** Thinking that you are spiritually
    superior because of your experiences creates another form of ego.

4.  **Don't chase experiences:** When you become attached to spiritual
    experiences, meditation can become another form of desire.

5.  **Investigate the experiencer:** Rather than becoming fascinated
    with what you experience, investigate who is experiencing it.

## 10. The Essence of Chapter IV

I would summarize the entire chapter in one statement:

**Don't become fascinated by what the mind experiences. Discover the
awareness to which every experience appears.**

You might meditate for years and experience unusual visions, sensations,
or powers.

But if anger, fear, possessiveness, pride, and attachment continue to
dominate your life, you should not mistake those experiences for
liberation.

Conversely, you don't need visions, supernatural powers, or
extraordinary meditation experiences to investigate your true nature.

A simple, sincere investigation into the source of the 'I' is sufficient
to begin.

### One thought to carry into your daily life

Whenever something unusual happens during meditation, or even during
ordinary life, remember:

*"This is something I am experiencing. But who is the one experiencing
it?"*

That question brings you back to the central teaching of Ramana
Maharshi.

**The objective is not to collect extraordinary experiences. It is to
understand the experiencer.**
