# Chapter III: Destiny and Free-Will
**Pages 27–37**

This chapter explores one of the most interesting questions in spiritual philosophy:

**Are we controlling our lives, or is everything already determined by destiny?**

Ramana Maharshi approaches this question from a deeper perspective. He suggests that both destiny and free will operate at the level of the individual ego. When we discover our true Self, we begin to understand that neither destiny nor free will defines who we really are.

Let's break this down.

## 1. What is Destiny?

Destiny refers to the events and circumstances that come into our lives as a result of past karma.

For example:
- Where you are born.
- Your family and upbringing.
- Certain opportunities that come your way.
- Unexpected successes and failures.
- Circumstances that you cannot easily change.

According to this philosophy, some aspects of our lives are influenced by past actions and their consequences.

However, this doesn't mean we should become passive or stop making efforts.

**Destiny determines certain circumstances, but how we respond to them is also important.**

### Example

Imagine you work hard for a promotion, but your company promotes someone else.

You cannot change the decision that has already happened.

But you can choose how to respond:
- Become frustrated and blame everyone.
- Accept the situation and understand what went wrong.
- Improve your skills and prepare for the next opportunity.

The event may be beyond your control, but your response becomes part of your present experience and action.

## 2. What is Free Will?

Free will is our ability to make choices and take action.

For example:
- You decide to learn something new.
- You choose to exercise every morning.
- You decide to control your anger.
- You choose whether to react positively or negatively to a difficult situation.

But here comes an interesting question:

**If our thoughts, desires and decisions are influenced by our past experiences, habits and karma, how much freedom do we actually have?**

Ramana takes this question further.

He suggests that as long as we identify ourselves with the mind and ego, we experience ourselves as individuals who make decisions and perform actions.

Therefore, free will and destiny belong to the same level of existence: the individual identity.

The deeper spiritual question is not simply whether we have free will, but:

**Who is the person claiming to exercise free will?**

## 3. Two Types of Intentions: Binding and Liberating

This is an important distinction in the chapter.

Not all actions have the same spiritual significance.

### A. Binding intentions

These are actions driven by ego, attachment and personal expectations.

Examples:
- Working only to become more powerful than others.
- Helping someone because you expect something in return.
- Becoming unhappy when people don't appreciate your efforts.
- Constantly comparing your achievements with others.

Here, the mind becomes attached to the outcome.

When the outcome is positive, you feel happy.

When the outcome is negative, you feel disappointed.

Your happiness becomes dependent on external circumstances.

### B. Liberating intentions

These are actions performed without excessive attachment to personal rewards.

Examples:
- Working sincerely because the work deserves your attention.
- Helping someone without expecting anything in return.
- Learning because knowledge itself is valuable.
- Performing your responsibilities without constantly worrying about recognition.

You still act, make decisions and work towards goals.

But your inner happiness is not completely dependent on the outcome.

**The difference is not necessarily in the action itself, but in the attachment and ego behind the action.**

## 4. What is Real Willpower?

One of the most practical teachings in this chapter is the meaning of willpower.

We generally think that willpower means:

- Having the determination to achieve something.
- Overcoming obstacles.
- Making things happen according to our expectations.
- Never accepting failure.

But the spiritual perspective is different.

**True willpower is the ability to remain mentally balanced in both success and failure.**

Let's understand this with an example.

Imagine you start a business.

You invest money, time and effort.

After two years, the business fails.

There are two possible responses.

**Response 1: Ego-driven reaction**

- I failed.
- My life is ruined.
- People will think I am incapable.
- Why did this happen to me?
- I cannot accept this failure.

Here, your identity is attached to the result.

**Response 2: Equanimous reaction**

- I made an effort.
- The result was not what I expected.
- Let me understand what went wrong.
- I will learn from this experience.
- I will continue doing what is appropriate.

You are not denying the failure.

You are simply refusing to let failure destroy your inner stability.

This is what equanimity means.

It doesn't mean you shouldn't feel emotions. It means you don't become completely controlled by them.

## 5. If Everything Is Destiny, Why Should We Make an Effort?

This is probably the most important practical question.

Suppose everything is predetermined.

Then why should we:
- Work hard?
- Study?
- Build businesses?
- Improve our health?
- Develop our knowledge?
- Make decisions?

Ramana's teaching doesn't encourage laziness or inaction.

You should continue performing your responsibilities.

The important difference is that you should not develop the belief that you have absolute control over every outcome.

For example:

You want to become an expert in Artificial Intelligence.

You can:
1. Study neural networks.
2. Learn Python and PyTorch.
3. Build practical projects.
4. Experiment with different models.
5. Improve your understanding.

These are actions within your present experience.

But you cannot guarantee exactly when you will become an expert or what opportunities will come your way.

Your responsibility is to make sincere efforts, not to guarantee the future.

**Work with full involvement, but don't become mentally imprisoned by the result.**

## 6. The Deeper Teaching: Who Is the Doer?

Now we come to the central teaching of Ramana Maharshi.

Normally, we think:

- I am doing this.
- I am achieving this.
- I am failing.
- I am controlling my future.
- I am responsible for everything happening in my life.

Ramana asks us to investigate the identity of this "I".

Who is this I?

Is it the body?

Is it the mind?

Is it the collection of thoughts, memories, experiences and desires?

When you investigate the source of the individual "I", Ramana teaches that the ego or separate sense of individuality can dissolve.

What remains is the true Self.

From this perspective, actions can continue even without the ego's constant claim:

"I am the independent doer, and everything must happen according to my wishes."

This is not about abandoning responsibilities.

It is about abandoning the false sense of ownership and absolute control.

## 7. Practical Application in Everyday Life

Let's translate the chapter into five practical principles.

| Situation | Ordinary reaction | Spiritual understanding |
|---|---|---|
| Success | I achieved everything through my ability. | I made an effort; I need not become arrogant. |
| Failure | I am a failure. | The result was unsuccessful, but it doesn't define me. |
| Unexpected problems | Why is this happening to me? | Accept the situation and respond intelligently. |
| Other people's behaviour | I must control how others behave. | I cannot control everyone. |
| Future uncertainty | Everything must happen according to my plan. | Make sincere efforts without demanding guaranteed results. |

## 8. Connection to Self-Inquiry

The ultimate purpose of this chapter is not simply to teach us how to accept destiny.

It is to take us beyond the entire question of destiny and free will.

Consider this thought:

"I must control my destiny."

Instead of immediately accepting this thought, investigate:

- Who wants to control destiny?
- Who is afraid of failure?
- Who wants success?
- Who is worried about the future?

The answer initially appears to be "I".

Ramana asks us to investigate this "I" rather than continuously following its thoughts and desires.

This is the essence of self-inquiry (*Atma Vichara*).

As the identification with the ego weakens, the struggle between destiny and free will also loses its importance.

## 9. Five Key Takeaways

1. **Destiny:** Certain circumstances arise through causes beyond our present control.
2. **Free Will:** At the individual level, we continue to make choices and perform actions.
3. **Detachment:** Perform your responsibilities without becoming excessively attached to the results.
4. **Willpower:** Real strength is maintaining mental balance through both success and failure.
5. **Self-Realization:** True freedom is not controlling everything in life; it is discovering the Self beyond the individual doer.

### One thought to remember from this chapter

> Make every effort sincerely, accept the results gracefully, and investigate the one who claims ownership of every action.

## Connection with Chapter II: Life, Death and Rebirth

There is a beautiful progression between these two chapters.

**Chapter II – Life, Death and Rebirth**

You are not merely the body that is born and dies. The true Self is beyond bodily existence.

**Chapter III – Destiny and Free-Will**

You are not merely the individual who performs actions, experiences success and failure, and struggles to control destiny.

**The common message:**

Both chapters gradually move our attention away from the external identity towards the underlying Self.

The body is not your ultimate identity. The individual doer is not your ultimate identity.

The question that remains is:

**Who am I, really?**
